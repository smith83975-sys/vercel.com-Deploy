The Wizard of Oz and silver: the money allegory explained

A fun one for silver fans: in L. Frank Baum's 1900 book The Wonderful Wizard of Oz, Dorothy doesn't wear ruby slippers. She wears silver shoes, walks a road paved with yellow (gold) bricks, and travels through a land called Oz, the abbreviation for ounce. A popular theory says the whole story is a fable about America's 1890s fight over gold and silver money. It's a fun theory, so here's the story and the fine print.

The 1890s: gold vs silver

For most of the 1800s, US law let both metals serve as money. From 1834 the Mint's legal ratio was 16 to 1: 16 ounces of silver were worth one ounce of gold (Congressional Research Service).

Then came the Coinage Act of 1873, signed by President Grant on February 12, 1873 (Coinage Act of 1873). It quietly dropped the standard silver dollar from the list of US coins. As silver prices fell, that kept the country on gold. Silver supporters called it the “Crime of '73.”

Falling prices in the late 1800s squeezed indebted farmers in the South and West, who had to repay loans with crops that sold for less. Their answer was free silver: let anyone bring silver to the Mint and have it coined, without limit, at 16 to 1. More money, they argued, would lift prices and ease debts.

On July 9, 1896, at the Democratic National Convention in Chicago, a 36-year-old former congressman from Nebraska, William Jennings Bryan, gave one of the most famous speeches in American political history. It ended: “You shall not press down upon the brow of labor this crown of thorns, you shall not crucify mankind upon a cross of gold” (Teaching American History). The next day the convention nominated him for president, on a platform demanding free coinage of silver at 16 to 1 (1896 Democratic platform).

Bryan lost. In November 1896, Republican William McKinley won 271 electoral votes to Bryan's 176 (American Presidency Project). The Gold Standard Act of March 1900 made gold official (CRS), the same year Baum's book came out in Chicago.

The ending is the punchline. After a whole trip down the golden road to a wizard, Glinda tells Dorothy: “Your Silver Shoes will carry you over the desert. If you had known their power you could have gone back to your Aunt Em the very first day you came to this country” (Baum, 1900, Project Gutenberg). For silverites, that's the message in one line.

Where the theory came from

The reading was first laid out by Henry M. Littlefield, a New York high school history teacher, in “The Wizard of Oz: Parable on Populism,” American Quarterly, Spring 1964 (JSTOR). He meant it as a teaching tool, writing that the parallels “furnish a teaching mechanism which is guaranteed to reach any level of student” (quoted by historian David B. Parker, 1994).

In 1990 economist Hugh Rockoff took it further in “The ‘Wizard of Oz’ as a Monetary Allegory,” Journal of Political Economy (JPE, vol. 98). He found more clues, like the seven passages and three flights of stairs Dorothy climbs in the Wizard's palace (7 and 3, for 1873), and argued that the free-silver side “had a strong argument.” The ounce pun came from historian Richard Jensen, who noted in 1971 that anyone who knew the slogan “16 to 1” would spot Oz as oz. (Parker)

The honest caveat

Baum never said any of this. In his introduction he called the book a fairy tale written “solely to please children of today.” Baum scholar Michael Patrick Hearn pointed to Baum's Republican editorials and an 1896 poem cheering McKinley and “honest money.” In a February 7, 1992 letter to the New York Times, Littlefield himself agreed that “there is no basis in fact to consider Baum a supporter of turn-of-the-century Populist ideology,” while still seeing the book as a parable on Populism (NYT letter).

Fair summary: the allegory is a popular, well-argued reading and a great way to learn the 1890s money fight, not proof of what Baum meant.

From 16 to 1 to today

The free-silver crowd wanted silver fixed at 16 ounces per ounce of gold. Today no law fixes the ratio; markets set it. FAQ

Is The Wizard of Oz really about gold and silver?

It is a popular theory, not a proven fact. Henry Littlefield proposed the reading in 1964 and Hugh Rockoff expanded it in 1990, but L. Frank Baum never said the book was an allegory, and historians still debate it.

Were Dorothy's shoes silver or ruby?

Silver. In Baum's 1900 book they are silver shoes. MGM changed them to ruby for the 1939 film to show off Technicolor, according to the Smithsonian's National Museum of American History.

What does 16 to 1 mean?

It was the legal ratio of silver to gold at the US Mint: 16 ounces of silver were worth one ounce of gold. Free-silver supporters in 1896 wanted unlimited coinage of silver at that ratio.

What was the Crime of '73?

A nickname silver supporters gave the Coinage Act of 1873, which dropped the standard silver dollar from US coinage and moved the country toward a gold standard.
