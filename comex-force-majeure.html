COMEX force majeure, explained

Force majeure is French for "superior force." It's a clause in many contracts that lets a party delay or skip its obligations when extraordinary events outside its control make performance impossible, such as war, a natural disaster, a pandemic lockdown or a government order. It doesn't cancel the contract by default, and it isn't a penalty-free way out of a bad deal.

What it would mean at COMEX

COMEX is the CME Group exchange where gold and silver futures trade. Most contracts are closed before expiry, but a holder of an expiring contract can take delivery of metal from a COMEX-approved vault. The exchange's rulebook includes emergency and force majeure provisions. In a genuine emergency, these could allow delays or other changes to physical delivery instead of counting as a default. How any case is handled depends on the exchange's rules and decisions at the time.

In plain terms, force majeure is about performance being impossible because of an outside event, for example vaults being inaccessible or transport being shut down. A tight market or high prices are not the same thing.

What it is not

  • It isn't a prediction. Explaining the clause doesn't mean we expect it to be used.
  • It isn't the same as a margin increase, position limits or a trading halt, which are other tools exchanges can use.
  • It isn't the same as cash settlement, which some other contracts use by design.

Where to check official notices

CME Group posts exchange notices and its rulebook on its website. If you read a claim online that COMEX has declared force majeure, look for the notice on cmegroup.com before believing it.
